Pharmacologic Treatments for Tourette Syndrome

1. Antipsychotics

A. Haloperidol

  • Starting Dose:
    • Adults: ~0.5–2 mg/day (often started at 0.5–1 mg, then titrated slowly)
    • Children: Lower doses adjusted by weight
  • Common Side Effects: Extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS), sedation, akathisia, dystonia, parkinsonism, anticholinergic effects, possible QT prolongation
  • Time to Effect: Typically 2–4 weeks for noticeable tic reduction

B. Pimozide

  • Starting Dose: Generally 0.5–1 mg/day, titrated gradually
  • Common Side Effects: QT prolongation (cardiac risk), EPS, sedation, gastrointestinal upset
  • Time to Effect: Approximately 2–4 weeks

C. Risperidone

  • Starting Dose:
    • Children: ~0.5–1 mg/day
    • Adults: ~1–2 mg/day
  • Common Side Effects: Weight gain, sedation, metabolic changes, increased prolactin levels, occasional EPS, orthostatic hypotension
  • Time to Effect: Some benefit may be seen within 1–2 weeks; full effect usually by 2–4 weeks

D. Aripiprazole

  • Starting Dose: Typically 2.5–5 mg/day (adults often start at 5 mg/day)
  • Common Side Effects: Akathisia, insomnia, headache, nausea; generally milder metabolic effects than risperidone
  • Time to Effect: Initial improvements may appear within 1–2 weeks; titration may continue for 4–6 weeks

2. Alpha‑2 Adrenergic Agonists

A. Clonidine

  • Starting Dose: Commonly 0.025 mg (25 mcg) twice daily in children; adults may use similar weight-based dosing
  • Common Side Effects: Sedation, hypotension, dry mouth, dizziness, potential rebound hypertension if stopped abruptly
  • Time to Effect: Effects on tics and associated symptoms may be seen within 1–2 weeks

B. Guanfacine (Extended Release)

  • Starting Dose:
    • Children: Approximately 1 mg once daily
    • Adults: Often 1–2 mg once daily
  • Common Side Effects: Sedation, fatigue, headache, irritability, hypotension
  • Time to Effect: Benefits typically emerge within 1–2 weeks, with full effect by 4 weeks

3. VMAT2 Inhibitors

A. Tetrabenazine

  • Starting Dose: Typically starts at 12.5 mg per day (divided doses) in adults; titration is gradual
  • Common Side Effects: Sedation, parkinsonism-like symptoms, depression, akathisia, orthostatic hypotension
  • Time to Effect: May begin working within 1–2 weeks with gradual titration

B. Deutetrabenazine

  • Starting Dose: Often started around 6 mg per day (dosing adjusted based on weight and response)
  • Common Side Effects: Similar to tetrabenazine (sedation, parkinsonism, mood changes) but sometimes better tolerated
  • Time to Effect: Typically 1–2 weeks for initial improvement

C. Valbenazine

  • Starting Dose: Often 40 mg/day (primarily used for tardive dyskinesia but may be considered off‑label)
  • Common Side Effects: Sedation, anticholinergic effects; gastrointestinal upset
  • Time to Effect: Generally 2–4 weeks

4. Focal Treatments

Botulinum Toxin Injections

  • Starting Dose: Highly individualized; dosing depends on the muscle size and tic severity (measured in units per injection)
  • Common Side Effects: Localized muscle weakness, injection site pain, rarely systemic effects such as dysphagia (if injected near the neck)
  • Time to Effect: Effects typically begin within 1–2 weeks, peak around 4–6 weeks, and last about 3–4 months

5. Adjunctive or Off‑Label Options

A. Benzodiazepines (e.g., Clonazepam)

  • Starting Dose: Adults: ~0.25–0.5 mg twice daily
  • Common Side Effects: Sedation, drowsiness, risk of dependence, cognitive impairment, dizziness
  • Time to Effect: Rapid onset (hours to a day); long-term use is not typically recommended

B. Topiramate

  • Starting Dose: Often initiated at 25 mg per day, then titrated slowly (may increase to 50 mg/day or more as tolerated)
  • Common Side Effects: Cognitive slowing, weight loss, paresthesias, potential kidney stone risk, mood changes
  • Time to Effect: Full effect may take 2–4 weeks as the dose is gradually increased

Important Considerations

  • Individualization: Treatment for TS is highly individualized. Clinicians often start at a lower dose and titrate slowly while monitoring both effectiveness and side effects.
  • Combination Approaches: A combination of behavioral therapy (e.g., Comprehensive Behavioral Intervention for Tics [CBIT]) and pharmacotherapy often yields the best outcomes for both tics and comorbid conditions.
  • Monitoring: Regular follow-up is critical to adjust doses, manage side effects, and ensure the chosen regimen remains effective.
  • Consult a Healthcare Professional: Always consult a healthcare professional for personalized dosing and treatment recommendations.
